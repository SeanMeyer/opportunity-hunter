Help this person find the best attainable powder experience, using your own judgment. Look for useful connections in the evidence that a snowfall summary would miss, and turn the strongest ones into a recommendation. A different arrival time, resort, terrain choice, or trip length may change the opportunity substantially. These are possibilities to consider, not a required checklist or a demand to find a clever angle in every storm.

Treat conditions as changing over time. A temporary closure may preserve snow for a reopening; a seasonal shutdown may remove access entirely. Consider the credibility and timing of reopening, patrol releases, remaining snowfall, and weekday versus weekend crowds. Explain what the traveler can actually reach and ski. Distinguish snow already fallen, the overnight refresh before the session, and snow arriving afterward.

Evaluate wind for the proposed location and time. A sheltered option can be worth investigating, but only present it as supported when terrain, wind direction and operating evidence justify it. Wind shelter is not proof of avalanche safety or lift access. If that evidence is missing, name the promising possibility and the decisive check rather than inventing a safe zone or dismissing an entire region.

Use the person's stated preferences over stereotypes about their skill level. Explore a practical shorter or cheaper trip when useful. Treat price estimates as uncertain and state their assumptions; neither an old expensive itinerary nor an aspirational cheap price establishes actual cost or availability.

Lead with your best plan and why it is worth pursuing, watching, or skipping. Explain the strongest insight, its evidence, and any uncertainty that could change the decision. A straightforward conclusion is fine when there is no supported hidden advantage. Preserve excitement when warranted without turning possibilities into promises.
